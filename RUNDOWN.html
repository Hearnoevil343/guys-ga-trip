0. Trip at a glance

Dates: Arrive Vogel State Park Thu Oct 15, 1:00 PM. Depart Wed Oct 21, noon. Keep the night of Oct 20 (group returns from the overnight Tue evening).

Site limits (walk-in site P): 6 people max, 2 tents max, 2 vehicles at the site + overflow parking nearby for the rest.

Key date warnings:
  • Muzzleloader (primitive weapons) deer season: Oct 10–16 — overlaps your first two days.

3. The overnight

Trip-lead decision: PRIMARY is Three Forks/Noontootla Creek. BACKUP is Rock Creek dispersed area (Fannin). Best dates: Mon–Tue Oct 19–20. This overrides all three research files' internal rankings (see §12).

PRIMARY — Three Forks / Noontootla Creek

Drive route from Vogel: North on US-19/129 to Suches (GA-60), then Aska Rd 13.5 mi to its end, right on Newport Rd 4.5 mi, left on Doublehead Gap Rd, cross a concrete bridge, gravel 0.3 mi to FS Road 58, right (east) 5.3 mi to Three Forks. High clearance recommended for the last stretch. ~45 min (estimated, not live-routed).

Trailhead/parking: No formal lot — park along FS Road 58 at Three Forks. Coordinates: 34.6636, -84.1842.

Mile-by-mile notes: Three Forks is the confluence of Stover, Chester, and Long Creeks forming Noontootla Creek — also an AT/Benton MacKaye Trail junction. Exact one-way mileage to a quiet pan site is UNVERIFIED (~1–2 mi estimated) — short, easy overnight well within the group's pace.

Camp location: Established sites cluster right at Three Forks (car-camp/thru-hiker traffic) — for solitude, camp a short distance upstream or downstream instead. No separate marked tent-pad coordinates found.

Water sources: Three Forks/Noontootla Creek — reliable, perennial; treat before drinking.

Pan reaches: The Three Forks confluence and Noontootla Creek downstream. Avoid panning right at Long Creek Falls (slick rock).

Gold record: The USFS's first Georgia land purchase (1911) was a 31,000-acre tract explicitly named for the "Rock Creek and Noontootly Creek area" of Fannin/Gilmer/Lumpkin/Union counties. A separate mining-claims index lists a "Hackney Placer" and mining along "Noontootly Creek" — the strongest creek-specific citation of any overnight candidate researched.

Why the Coosa Backcountry Trail is not the gold route

The Coosa Backcountry Trail (trailhead inside Vogel, near cabin #7) was the top pick in the earliest overnight research — zero drive time, a documented historic producer nearby (the Coosa Mine, reportedly $2M+ in gold). That pick has been corrected: the trail does not actually touch Coosa Creek, and no documented gold has been found along the trail itself — the historic Coosa Mine is a separate location about 4 miles south of Blairsville, off the trail entirely, with UNVERIFIED public/legal access status. The trail crosses Wolf Creek and Wolf Branch Creek, and its eastern ~1.25 miles sits inside Blood Mountain Wilderness, where panning is banned outright.

Bottom line: still a good hike — free permit from the Vogel visitor center, ~12.7-mi full loop (too long for this group's pace) or a shorter out-and-back — but not a panning destination. List it as a plain-hike option for anyone who wants a day off from creek work.

6. How to find gold here

The geology, in plain English

North Georgia's gold sits in the Georgia Gold Belt — Vogel is camped inside the northern edge of the Dahlonega gold belt, not just near it. Gold started as quartz veins deep underground, laced with tiny bits of gold and pyrite ("fool's gold"). As erosion slowly wears the mountains down, gold doesn't dissolve like most rock — it's chemically inert and about 19x denser than water (roughly 7x denser than the quartz around it) — so it settles out and works into stream gravel as it's freed. That's placer gold: flakes and small bits already freed from the rock and carried downstream.

How to read a creek

  • Inside bends — the slower side of a curve, opposite the cut bank.
  • Behind boulders — the calm pocket just downstream of anything sticking up.
  • Bedrock cracks and potholes — exposed bedrock is a natural riffle.
  • Under a hard clay layer ("false bedrock") if you hit one before true bedrock.
  • Along old flood lines — a slightly higher, older gravel bar above the current waterline.
  • Under moss mats and tree roots at the water's edge.
  • Best single tell: exposed bedrock rather than deep loose gravel.

Panning, step by step

  1. Scoop from the bottom few inches of gravel, closest to bedrock/clay, not just the surface.
  2. Break up clay clumps by hand, toss the big rocks after a glance for anything shiny/heavy.
  3. Submerge the pan and shake side to side, keeping it level and under water so heavy material settles to the bottom.
  4. Tilt slightly away from you and gently wash the top layer off with small forward-and-back motions.
  5. Repeat until you're left with a small amount of black sand.
  6. Pick through the black sand, tilting and swirling in a little water — gold shows as tiny bright, flat specks.
  7. Transfer finds to a snuffer bottle (tiny flakes) or straight into a vial.

Indicator minerals to know

MineralHow to spot it
Black sand (magnetite/ilmenite)Heavy, concentrates like gold — a solid black line means you're in the right layer
GarnetSmall red/purple grains, another heavy-mineral tracer
Pyrite ("fool's gold")Brassy, cubic crystals, brittle — shatters instead of flattening under pressure
Milky quartzSource-vein material — means an eroding vein is somewhere upstream

Cleanup, snuffer bottles, and vials

Keep a plastic tub as an evening "pan-out station" — re-pan concentrates with better light, use a magnet wand to pull out black sand (magnetite is magnetic, gold isn't). Use the snuffer bottle to suck up small flakes, squirt into a labeled vial.

Managing expectations: this is flakes, not nuggets. Georgia placer gold in this belt is mostly fine flour gold and small flakes — genuine visible nuggets are rare even historically. A good day is a few visible flakes in the vial, not a jar full of gold. Frame it as a fun outdoor skill first, treasure hunt a distant second.

Hunting season safety

Muzzleloader/primitive weapons Oct 10–16; firearms Oct 17 – Jan 10, 2027; archery open the whole trip. Blaze orange (500 sq in above the waist) is legally required for hunters during primitive/firearms seasons on WMA/federal land — not legally required for non-hunters, but wear it anyway, group-wide, given how much NF/WMA land the itinerary covers. Vogel itself is not huntable.

Bear / food storage

Use Vogel's steel bear-proof hang posts at site P — never leave food/coolers/trash out overnight or in a tent. On the overnight, hang food or use a bear-resistant bag/canister (Ursack on the gear list). Note: the AT's mandatory hard-sided-canister rule (Jarrard Gap–Neel Gap) only applies March 1–June 1, so it doesn't legally apply here — but bear-resistant storage is still standard practice.

Cold-water safety

Creek water is cold in October (likely 50s°F). Insulated waterproof gloves are not optional. Neoprene socks under water shoes, never hiking boots. Change into dry socks/shoes immediately after any creek session — wet feet plus 30s°F nights is the biggest cold risk on this trip.

Weather

Open-Meteo historical data (Oct 15–21, 2020–2025): Vogel-area lows averaged 39–48°F per year, with the coldest night 29°F (October 2022); highs mostly 60s–70s. In 2025, highs ranged 64–77°F and lows 39–53°F. NOAA CPC October–December 2026 outlook (issued Sept 17) favors warmer and wetter than normal — plan for rain. A 30°F-rated sleeping bag or quilt is sufficient with a puffy layer + base layers (20°F-rated gear only needed for cold sleepers). Rain gear matters most. Water filter and creek water remain cold (50s°F) — keep the filter in your sleeping bag as a cold-snap precaution; panning gloves and neoprene socks stay standard. Flash flood risk is real in narrow gorges after heavy rain — check weather.gov close to the trip.

Cold-water panning tips (recap)

Insulated gloves are not optional — dip, work fast, dry and rewarm between pans. Trail runners + 3mm neoprene socks beat boots for creek work (boots hold water and don't dry); use water shoes/old sneakers. Kneel on a foam pad, not bare gravel. Waders are not needed — all panning here is shin-deep hand work with a pan. Keep a strict two-bag system: one dry bag that never leaves the tent vestibule wet-handed, one wet bag for creek gear. Line the pack with a contractor-bag liner. Reserve one pair of wool socks only ever worn dry, inside the sleeping bag. Garrett Gravity Trap 14" is the community-favorite pan. Food storage = Ursack (Southeast standard).

9. Campfire history

America's first gold rush — twenty years before California. North Georgia's gold rush kicked off in 1828. Popular legend credits Benjamin Parks, who said he found gold on his birthday in 1828 near Dukes Creek. Competing legends credit Frank Logan (or an enslaved man working for him) or John Witheroods and a 3-ounce nugget. None of these stories has a contemporary document behind them — the earliest solid paper trail is an August 1, 1829 newspaper notice. Tell the Parks version, but flag it as legend, not fact.

4,000 miners on one creek. By 1830, reports claimed 4,000 miners working Yahoola Creek alone — the same creek at Yahoola Creek Park. This was, at the time, still legally Cherokee Nation land. The rush directly accelerated pressure for Cherokee removal — Georgia's land-lottery system started parceling out gold-bearing land in 1832, years before the forced march known as the Trail of Tears in 1838.

A U.S. Mint, right there in the mountains. Congress opened a branch mint in Dahlonega in 1838. Over 23 years it struck more than $6 million in gold coins, all marked with a "D." The mint closed with the Civil War in 1861 and never reopened; the building became the core of a hall at what's now the University of North Georgia, right on the square.

How the landscape remembers the rush. As easy placer gold ran out, mining companies turned to hydraulic mining — blasting hillsides apart with water cannons, notably around Dahlonega and Dukes Creek. That's why Smithgall Woods State Park bans mineral extraction today: rehabilitating trout habitat scoured out over a century ago. The Dahlonega Consolidated Gold Mining Company's stamp mill on Yahoola Creek — reportedly the largest ever built east of the Mississippi — was the industrial tail end of the boom.

"There's gold in them thar hills." Popularly tied to Georgia, sometimes credited to a Dahlonega Mint assayer trying to talk miners out of chasing the 1849 California rush — no solid contemporary source for the exact phrase was found. Good campfire line; say up front it's folklore with plausible Georgia roots.

Ties to your actual creeks this week: Yahoola Creek (the 4,000-miner creek and mill site), Dukes Creek (the discovery legend and the hydraulic-mining scars Smithgall now protects), Nimblewill/Wolf Creek/Auraria (boomtown-era diggings), Etowah River (Cherokee/Sixes lode mines and a still-visible 1800s mining tunnel).
