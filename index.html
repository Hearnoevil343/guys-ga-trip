The three creeks

Fri Oct 16 — Yahoola Creek Park (Dahlonega) · warm-up
  • Where: 1166 Captain McDonald Rd, paved lot, level walk of a few hundred feet to the creek. 38 min from Vogel.
  • Gold record: the most storied creek in the district. About 4,000 miners worked it in 1830 and a big stamp mill sat on its bank.
  • Expect: heavily worked for almost 200 years and panned by visitors. Fine flour gold and small flakes at best. This is the day to get your technique right.
  • Try first: gravel bars away from the mowed lawn frontage. Inside of bends, the downstream side of big rocks, and the bottom layer of gravel where it sits on clay or bedrock.
  • Status: county park. No written county rule either way: the county code and the park's posted rules do not mention panning. Not confirmed as allowed. Call Lumpkin Co. Parks & Rec 706-864-3622.
  • Park rules: park hours 7:30 AM-9:00 PM. No glass in the park: plastic vials only.
Sat Oct 17 — West Fork Wolf Creek · test pan, 2 h
  • Where: at and just below the FS 107 crossing (WOLF-X), where truck 1 parks. National Forest from the source down to 34.7906, -83.9190. Do not pan below that point. This is not a camp — night 1 is up the trail at Calf Stump Branch.
  • Also: last water a vehicle can reach. Fill 2 L each here — there is nothing on the 1,375 ft climb to Calf Stomp Gap.
  • Gold record: none. It is a guess from geology: the creek sits between the Coosa Creek placers and the old placers near Crumley Creek. Finding nothing here is a normal result.
  • Expect: a steep creek with bedrock. Little gravel, so the gold (if any) is in cracks, not in bars.
  • Try first: bedrock cracks that run across the current, the pocket at the foot of each small drop, and gravel packed behind boulders. Scrape cracks clean with the trowel tip and pan that.
  • Method: sample, do not settle. Two pans per spot, then move 20–30 yards. Only stay where a pan shows black sand plus color.
  • Status: allowed under the general National Forest rule; no creek-specific rule found. Ranger call still open (Blue Ridge RD 706-745-6928).
Sat–Mon Oct 17–19 — Calf Stump Branch · night 1, 1.5 h evening + 1.5 h morning
  • Where: where the Coosa Backcountry Trail crosses Calf Stump Branch, 0.4 mi past Calf Stomp Gap (34.78244, -83.95858). About 400 m from the nearest road.
  • Gold record: none. Like West Fork Wolf Creek this is geology, not history. Finding nothing is a normal result.
  • Expect: a small headwater branch. October is the driest month and the flow here is UNCONFIRMED — it may be a trickle.
  • Try first: bedrock cracks across the current and the pocket at the foot of each small drop. Pan downstream of wherever the group draws drinking water.
Sun–Mon Oct 18–19 — East Fork Coosa Creek · the main event
  • Gold record: the best of the three. Georgia Geological Survey Bulletin 19 (1909, pp. 237–239) says the Coosa Creek placers were mined for several miles from near the headwaters, with output estimated at half a million to a million pennyweights and purity up to .980. USGS lists the Coosa Creek Placer Mine as a past producer.
  • Hard limit: National Forest only from the source down to 34.80637, -83.95980. Below that is private. Never pan downstream of it.
  • Where the gold sits: Bulletin 19 says upper-creek gold is in "the bed of the creek only". Work the creek bed: bedrock cracks and the inside of bends. Skip the banks.
  • Why these stops: the creek is steep at its source, flattens near 34.798, -83.976, then steepens again below the two confluences. Fast water that slows down drops its gold, and a side creek joining adds another drop zone.

Cheat sheet

Reading a creek: where gold stops

Gold is about 19 times heavier than water and 6–7 times heavier than ordinary rock. It drops wherever the current slows, and it works its way down to the bottom.

  • Inside of bends. The slow side. Dig at the upstream end of the gravel bar.
  • Behind boulders and logs. The calm pocket on the downstream side.
  • Bedrock cracks. Best when they run across the current like a riffle. Clean them to the bottom.
  • Foot of a drop. Where a chute or small falls flattens into a pool, at the tail of the pool rather than the deep middle.
  • Below a confluence. Where a side creek joins.
  • Moss and roots at the high-water line. Moss on rocks traps fine gold in floods. Rinse it into the pan; do not strip the bank.
  • Go deep. The top few inches of loose gravel are usually empty. The pay is in the tight layer on bedrock or clay.
  • Picture the creek in flood. That is when gold moves. Draw the straightest line between inside bends: gold travels and drops along it.
How to pan, step by step
  1. Fill the pan about three-quarters with gravel from the bottom of your hole.
  2. Sink it in calm water a few inches deep. Break up clay and clumps with your fingers until the water runs through everything.
  3. Pick out the big rocks. Rinse each one over the pan first. Glance at them for quartz.
  4. Shake hard, flat and under water, side to side. This is the step that matters: it sends the gold to the bottom. Shake more than feels necessary.
  5. Tilt and wash. Tip the pan slightly away from you and let water sweep the top layer of light sand over the lip.
  6. Level, re-shake, repeat. Every few washes, go flat and shake again so the gold stays down.
  7. Slow down at the black sand. When only a cup or two of heavy material is left, keep an inch of clean water in the pan and swirl gently to check for nuggets and pieces you can pick out by hand.
  8. Collect. Snuffer bottle for flakes, into the vial. A dry fingertip also picks up a flake.

A pan takes a beginner 10–15 minutes. When sampling, be quick and rough; when you find color, slow down and be careful.

Pin test and streak test: USGS, What is Fool's Gold? Gold dents like soft lead and leaves a golden yellow streak on unglazed porcelain; pyrite and chalcopyrite leave a dark green to black streak, mica a white one.

Working as a group of 5
  • Spread out to sample. One person per spot type (bend, boulder, bedrock, confluence), two pans each, then compare.
  • Follow the color. When a spot shows gold, everyone moves to that line and works it upstream.
  • Take the concentrates home. Stop each pan at the black sand, dump it in one shared bucket or bag, and do the careful final panning in a tub at camp.
  • Cold water: gloves on, take turns, and nobody pans alone out of sight on the hike days.

2. Georgia law for taking crayfish

License for crayfish: UNCONFIRMED. The license page does not say whether taking crayfish needs a license. The general rule is "Anglers age 16 and older must have a current Georgia fishing license in their possession" (https://www.eregulations.com/georgia/fishing/license-requirements). Safe play: the two licensed people do the taking (my assumption).

Protected crayfish and burrows (https://www.eregulations.com/georgia/fishing/general-regulations): - Georgia protects 20 crayfish: "58 fishes, 10 salamanders, one frog, one snail, 27 mussels, 20 crayfish" - If you catch a protected species: "please release it unharmed immediately." - The bait exception: "all native crayfish, including state protected species, can be used as bait". It holds only "as long as they are not harvested from burrows." - This exception covers bait only. Eating a protected species is not covered, so release crayfish at Yahoola (Lumpkin, two Threatened species). That advice is my assumption.

Moving live crayfish: "Bait species should never be transferred between water ways." (same page). That page has no separate rule naming crayfish transport; this is the only line found.

Trap rules. Georgia has no crayfish-trap rule in the guide. The minnow-trap rules (https://www.eregulations.com/georgia/fishing/fishing-methods): - Size: "less than 24 inches in length, 18 inches in width" (rectangular trap) - Throat: "The throat opening must be no larger than 1 inch" - Tag: "Each minnow trap must have an attached tag or float bearing the name and address of trap owner." - Number: "An individual may deploy up to 2 traps at any time." - Where: "In addition to designated trout waters, minnow seines and traps may not be used" (the page then lists other banned waters). So minnow traps are barred in trout waters. - Trout rules say the same: "Seining or trapping bait-fish is not allowed in any trout stream." (https://www.eregulations.com/georgia/fishing/trout-fishing) - Checking: "Anglers are encouraged to check all gear within 24 hours" (general-regulations page) - Whether a crawdad trap counts as a "minnow trap" under these rules: UNCONFIRMED. Every creek on this trip is trout water (section 4), so ask DNR before setting a trap. DNR's Wildlife Conservation Section is listed as "(706) 557-3213" (general-regulations page).

Bait you may use / daily limit for crayfish: UNCONFIRMED. No crayfish bag limit was found. The minnow-trap limit "Up to 50 nongame fish 5 inches in length or less" covers fish, not crayfish (fishing-methods page).

Catching by hand: UNCONFIRMED. The guide's hand-capture rule covers fish (noodling). I found no line on hand-catching crayfish.
